Minimax Regret, Hurwicz, and Laplace Criteria

When the possible states of nature are known but their probabilities are not, a decision maker may seek to minimize missed opportunities, balance optimistic and conservative outcomes, or give each state equal weight.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the lesson, you should be able to:

  1. Construct opportunity-loss (regret) matrices from payoff and cost tables.
  2. Apply the Minimax Regret (Savage) criterion.
  3. Apply the Hurwicz criterion using a coefficient of optimism.
  4. Apply the Laplace criterion and explain its equal-weight assumption.
  5. Compare the selected actions and interpret disagreements.

1. Cricket decision: Selecting a batter

A captain must select one batter for the final overs. The opposition may use one of four bowling approaches. The table gives hypothetical runs scored. Higher values are preferred. No probabilities are supplied.

Batter Pace-heavy Spin-heavy Mixed Slower-ball-heavy
Batter A 36 14 22 10
Batter B 28 25 24 20
Batter C 22 30 27 18
Batter D 40 12 16 8

Recall Week 03: Maximax chooses Batter D (best case \(40\)), while Maximin chooses Batter B (worst case \(20\)).

2. Minimax Regret (Savage criterion)

Regret measures the payoff forgone by not choosing the best action for the state that actually occurs. For payoff \(P_{ij}\):

\[ R_{ij}=\max_k P_{kj}-P_{ij}. \]

The Minimax Regret rule is

\[ A^*=\arg\min_i\left\{\max_j R_{ij}\right\}. \]

Step 1: Best payoff for each state

State Pace-heavy Spin-heavy Mixed Slower-ball-heavy
Best available runs 40 30 27 20

Step 2: Regret table

For Batter A against pace-heavy bowling, regret is \(40-36=4\). Against spin-heavy bowling, it is \(30-14=16\).

Batter Pace-heavy Spin-heavy Mixed Slower-ball-heavy Maximum regret
Batter A 4 16 5 10 16
Batter B 12 5 3 0 12
Batter C 18 0 0 2 18
Batter D 0 18 11 12 18

Step 3: Choose the smallest maximum regret

\[ \min\{16,12,18,18\}=12. \]

Minimax Regret selects Batter B. Its maximum missed opportunity is 12 runs.

Note

Discussion: Does a regret of 12 mean that Batter B scores 12 runs? No. It is the largest difference between Batter B’s runs and the best runs achievable in the same state.

Minimax Regret and Maximin happen to choose the same batter here, but they are different rules. Maximin evaluates the smallest actual payoff. Minimax Regret evaluates the largest payoff forgone.

3. Hurwicz criterion (criterion of realism)

Hurwicz combines the best and worst possible outcomes using a coefficient of optimism, \(\alpha\), where \(0\leq\alpha\leq1\).

For a payoff matrix:

\[ H_i(\alpha)=\alpha\max_j P_{ij}+(1-\alpha)\min_j P_{ij}. \]

Choose the action with the largest \(H_i(\alpha)\). When \(\alpha=1\), this is Maximax. When \(\alpha=0\), it is Maximin.

The coefficient \(\alpha\) is not the probability of any state.

Worked example: \(\alpha = 0.6\)

For each batter, the maximum payoff receives weight 0.6 and the minimum payoff receives weight 0.4.

Batter Minimum (runs) Maximum (runs) Hurwicz score (runs)
Batter A 10 36 25.6
Batter B 20 28 24.8
Batter C 18 30 25.2
Batter D 8 40 27.2

For example, Batter A’s score is

\[ H_A(0.6)=0.6(36)+0.4(10)=25.6. \]

The corresponding calculations for the other batters are

\[ \begin{aligned} H_B(0.6)&=0.6(28)+0.4(20)=24.8,\\ H_C(0.6)&=0.6(30)+0.4(18)=25.2,\\ H_D(0.6)&=0.6(40)+0.4(8)=27.2. \end{aligned} \]

Hurwicz selects Batter D when \(\alpha=0.6\), because its score of 27.2 is the largest.

Sensitivity to optimism

Sensitivity analysis asks whether the preferred batter changes when the coefficient of optimism, \(\alpha\), changes. The payoffs in the table remain fixed. Only the relative importance given to the best and worst possible outcomes changes.

Recall the Hurwicz formula for payoffs:

\[ H_i(\alpha)=\alpha(\text{maximum payoff})+(1-\alpha)(\text{minimum payoff}), \qquad 0\leq\alpha\leq1. \]

When \(\alpha=0\), the decision is completely conservative (Maximin). When \(\alpha=1\), it is completely optimistic (Maximax). At \(\alpha=0.5\), the two extremes receive equal weight.

Step 1: Express each score as a function of \(\alpha\)

For Batter A, the minimum payoff is 10 runs and the maximum is 36 runs. Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} H_A(\alpha) &=\alpha(36)+(1-\alpha)(10)\\ &=36\alpha+10-10\alpha\\ &=10+26\alpha. \end{aligned} \]

Applying the same calculation to the remaining batters gives:

Batter Minimum Maximum Hurwicz score as a function of \(\alpha\)
A 10 36 \(H_A(\alpha)=10+26\alpha\)
B 20 28 \(H_B(\alpha)=20+8\alpha\)
C 18 30 \(H_C(\alpha)=18+12\alpha\)
D 8 40 \(H_D(\alpha)=8+32\alpha\)

Step 2: Compare the scores for different values of \(\alpha\)

\(\alpha\) Batter A Batter B Batter C Batter D Preferred batter
0.2 15.2 21.6 20.4 14.4 B
0.5 23.0 24.0 24.0 24.0 B, C and D (tie)
0.6 25.6 24.8 25.2 27.2 D
0.8 30.8 26.4 27.6 33.6 D

For low values of \(\alpha\), Batter B is attractive because its minimum payoff is relatively high (20 runs). As \(\alpha\) increases, Batter D becomes attractive because it has the largest maximum payoff (40 runs).

Step 3: Find where the decision changes

To identify the switching value, equate the Hurwicz scores for Batters B and D:

\[ \begin{aligned} 20+8\alpha &= 8+32\alpha\\ 12 &=24\alpha\\ \alpha &=0.5. \end{aligned} \]

At \(\alpha=0.5\), Batter C also has a score of 24, while Batter A has 23. Thus B, C and D tie at the switching value.

\[ \text{Preferred batter}=\begin{cases} \text{B}, & 0\leq\alpha<0.5,\\ \text{B, C and D (tie)}, & \alpha=0.5,\\ \text{D}, & 0.5<\alpha\leq1. \end{cases} \]

Interpretation

The coach’s degree of optimism is a subjective choice. Sensitivity analysis shows how much the recommendation depends on that choice. In this example, the decision changes at \(\alpha=0.5\). A more conservative coach selects Batter B, whereas a more optimistic coach selects Batter D.

ImportantDo not interpret \(\alpha\) as a probability

Increasing \(\alpha\) does not mean favorable bowling conditions have become more likely. It means the decision maker gives more weight to the best possible payoff.

4. Laplace criterion (equal-likelihood criterion)

Laplace assigns equal weights to all listed states when no probabilities are available. With \(m\) states:

\[ L_i=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}P_{ij}. \]

For payoffs, choose the largest \(L_i\). Here there are four states.

Batter Calculation Laplace score
A \((36+14+22+10)/4\) 20.50
B \((28+25+24+20)/4\) 24.25
C \((22+30+27+18)/4\) 24.25
D \((40+12+16+8)/4\) 19.00

Laplace produces a tie between Batters B and C.

The equal weights are an assumption of the criterion, not evidence that each bowling approach has probability \(0.25\). How states are defined can influence the result.

5. Compare all five decision rules

Criterion Principle Selected batter
Maximax Largest best-case payoff D
Maximin Largest worst-case payoff B
Minimax Regret Smallest maximum missed payoff B
Hurwicz (0.6 optimism) Weighted best and worst D
Laplace Highest equal-weight average B and C (tie)

Class discussion: If a poor performance eliminates the team, which criterion seems defensible? What if a very high score is essential? Explain without claiming any bowling approach is more probable.

6. Cost matrices: 100 m athletics example

A coach chooses a training plan for a 100 m sprinter. The following hypothetical finishing times in seconds are projected for two possible track conditions. Smaller values are better.

Training plan Dry track Wet track
Speed-focused 10.40 11.10
Balanced 10.65 10.75

6.1 Minimax Regret for costs

For a cost matrix, regret is the excess cost over the lowest cost in that state:

\[ R_{ij}=C_{ij}-\min_k C_{kj}. \]

Plan Dry-track regret Wet-track regret Maximum regret
Speed-focused 0 0.35 0.35
Balanced 0.25 0 0.25

Minimax Regret selects the balanced plan, with maximum avoidable time loss of \(0.25\) seconds.

6.2 Hurwicz for costs

For costs, weight the lowest cost as the optimistic outcome:

\[ H_i^{(C)}(\alpha)=\alpha\min_j C_{ij}+(1-\alpha)\max_j C_{ij}. \]

Choose the smallest score. With \(\alpha=0.6\):

  • Speed-focused: \(0.6(10.40)+0.4(11.10)=10.68\) seconds.
  • Balanced: \(0.6(10.65)+0.4(10.75)=10.69\) seconds.

Hurwicz selects the speed-focused plan.

6.3 Laplace for costs

  • Speed-focused: \((10.40+11.10)/2=10.75\) seconds.
  • Balanced: \((10.65+10.75)/2=10.70\) seconds.

Laplace selects the balanced plan, which has the smaller equal-weight average time.

Note

Payoffs versus costs

Criterion Payoff (higher is better) Cost (lower is better)
Minimax Regret State maximum minus payoff Cost minus state minimum
Hurwicz Maximize weighted best and worst payoffs Minimize weighted lowest and highest costs
Laplace Maximize equal-weight average Minimize equal-weight average

7. Worked example: Choosing a sports equipment supplier

Adapted from Example 9.4-4 in the reference textbook. The sporting context and amounts are hypothetical; the two-action, two-state cost structure is retained.

A national athletics training centre purchases specialist starting-block components each year. It has two options:

  • Supplier X: Continue using the existing imported components, currently costing LKR 100,000 per year. A possible international supply shortage could increase the annual price to four times its present value.
  • Supplier Y: Switch to a compatible locally sourced component package that provides the same required function and costs LKR 300,000 per year, regardless of whether the shortage occurs.

The centre does not know whether the shortage will occur, and no probabilities are available. Which supplier should it choose under the optimistic, pessimistic and Hurwicz criteria? Use a coefficient of optimism of \(\alpha=0.4\).

Step 1: Construct the conditional cost matrix

All amounts below are in LKR 1,000 per year. Lower costs are preferable.

Procurement decision Shortage occurs No shortage
\(A_1\): Continue with Supplier X 400 100
\(A_2\): Switch to Supplier Y 300 300

Step 2: Optimistic criterion (Minimin cost)

The optimistic decision considers the lowest possible cost for each alternative and chooses the smallest of these values.

\[ \min_j C_{1j}=\min(400,100)=100,\qquad \min_j C_{2j}=\min(300,300)=300. \]

Since \(100<300\), the optimistic decision is Supplier X, with a best-case annual cost of LKR 100,000.

In the textbook, Maximax is expressed for payoffs. For costs, the equivalent optimistic rule is Minimin, because a smaller cost is better. Equivalently, negate each cost to obtain a payoff and then apply Maximax.

Step 3: Pessimistic criterion (Minimax cost)

The pessimistic decision considers the highest possible cost of each alternative and chooses the smaller of these worst-case costs.

\[ \max_j C_{1j}=\max(400,100)=400,\qquad \max_j C_{2j}=\max(300,300)=300. \]

Since \(300<400\), the pessimistic decision is Supplier Y, with a worst-case annual cost of LKR 300,000. This is the cost-based form of the Maximin payoff criterion.

Step 4: Hurwicz criterion with \(\alpha=0.4\)

For costs, the coefficient of optimism weights the minimum cost, while \(1-\alpha\) weights the maximum cost:

\[ H_i^{(C)}(\alpha)=\alpha\min_j C_{ij}+(1-\alpha)\max_j C_{ij}. \]

The preferred alternative has the smallest Hurwicz cost score.

Supplier Minimum cost (LKR 1,000) Maximum cost (LKR 1,000) Hurwicz cost score (LKR 1,000)
X 100 400 280
Y 300 300 300

For Supplier X:

\[ H_X^{(C)}(0.4)=0.4(100)+0.6(400)=40+240=280. \]

For Supplier Y:

\[ H_Y^{(C)}(0.4)=0.4(300)+0.6(300)=120+180=300. \]

Because \(280<300\), Hurwicz selects Supplier X at \(\alpha=0.4\). The score of 280 corresponds to LKR 280,000, but it is a decision score, not a probability-weighted forecast of actual expenditure.

NoteAlternative solution using negative costs as payoffs

As in the reference textbook, define payoff as the negative of cost. In LKR 1,000, the payoff matrix becomes:

Supplier Shortage occurs No shortage
X -400 -100
Y -300 -300
  • Maximax payoff: X, because its largest payoff is \(-100\), compared with \(-300\) for Y.
  • Maximin payoff: Y, because its smallest payoff is \(-300\), compared with \(-400\) for X.
  • Hurwicz payoff, \(\alpha=0.4\): X gives \(0.4(-100)+0.6(-400)=-280\); Y gives \(-300\). Maximize the score, so choose X.

Both representations give exactly the same decisions. For a cost table, Minimin is optimistic and Minimax cost is pessimistic. For the negative-cost payoff table, these correspond to Maximax and Maximin, respectively.

Discussion: Would the centre necessarily choose Supplier X if its annual budget could not exceed LKR 350,000 under any circumstances? No. A strict budget constraint would rule out Supplier X because its shortage-state cost is LKR 400,000. A decision criterion does not replace feasibility constraints.

9. Common mistakes

  • Calculating regret relative to the best payoff in a row rather than the best payoff within each state.
  • Confusing regret with actual runs scored or finishing time.
  • Using payoff regret formulas without reversing the direction for costs.
  • Treating Hurwicz’s \(\alpha\) as a probability of a state.
  • Claiming that Laplace has estimated equal state probabilities.
  • Ignoring ties between actions.

10. Exit check (3 minutes)

Batter State 1 State 2
Batter A 20 8
Batter B 15 14
  1. Construct the regret matrix and select by Minimax Regret.
  2. Apply Hurwicz with \(\alpha=0.5\).
  3. Apply Laplace.
TipAnswers

Best statewise payoffs: \(20\) and \(14\).

Regret for A: \((0,6)\). Regret for B: \((5,0)\). Minimax Regret selects B because \(5<6\).

Hurwicz: \(H_A=0.5(20)+0.5(8)=14\); \(H_B=0.5(15)+0.5(14)=14.5\). Hurwicz selects B.

Laplace: \(L_A=14\) and \(L_B=14.5\). Laplace selects B.
