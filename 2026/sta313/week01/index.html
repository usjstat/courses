WEEK 01 · 60 MINUTES

Introduction to Statistical Decision Theory

A decision problem begins before any calculation is made. We first identify what can be chosen, what is uncertain, and what consequence follows from each action–state combination.

This first lesson focuses on the basic structure of a statistical decision problem. We will not yet use Maximax, Maximin, expected monetary value, or Bayesian decision rules. Those come in later weeks.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  1. explain why decision theory is useful when future outcomes are not fully known;
  2. distinguish a decision alternative from a state of nature;
  3. identify the consequence associated with an action–state combination;
  4. construct and interpret a simple payoff or loss table; and
  5. describe the four basic steps in the decision-theory approach.

Week 1 focus. Decision problems, alternatives, states of nature, consequences, payoff/loss tables, and the basic steps in the decision-theory approach.

1. Opening problem

A cricket decision before the theory

A cricket team is defending 12 runs in the final over.

The captain must choose one of three bowlers:

  • a fast bowler;
  • a spinner;
  • a medium-pace bowler.

The batting side may respond aggressively or may focus on rotating the strike.

Think for one minute

Question: Which bowler should the captain choose?

Do not calculate anything yet.

What information would you want before making the decision?

Class discussion

Possible answers include:

  • How has each bowler performed against aggressive batting?
  • How has each bowler performed when batters rotate the strike?
  • What is the pitch like?
  • Is one bowler more likely to concede boundaries?
  • Is one bowler better at limiting singles?
  • What is the estimated chance of successfully defending the target?

The key point is simple:

A decision is made now, but its consequence often depends on something that happens later and is not fully under our control.

That is the setting in which decision theory becomes useful.

2. What is statistical decision theory?

Statistical decision theory provides a structured way to make choices when outcomes depend partly on uncertain future conditions.

In this course, we will represent a decision problem using three basic ingredients:

  1. Decision alternatives
  2. States of nature
  3. Consequences

A fourth ingredient, available information, becomes increasingly important later when we study risk, Bayesian updating, and the value of information.

3. Anatomy of a decision problem

3.1 Decision alternatives

A decision alternative is an action available to the decision maker.

The decision maker can choose among the alternatives.

3.3 Consequences

A consequence is the result obtained when a particular alternative is chosen and a particular state of nature occurs.

The consequence might be expressed as:

  • runs conceded;
  • probability of winning;
  • points won;
  • race time;
  • expected medal probability;
  • injury risk;
  • expected performance score;
  • or another quantity relevant to the decision.

4. The four-step decision-theory approach

A useful decision-theory workflow is:

Step 1: List the viable alternatives

Identify the actions available to the decision maker.

Step 2: Identify the possible future events

List the relevant states of nature.

Step 3: Construct a payoff or loss table

Record the consequence for each action–state combination.

Step 4: Select an appropriate decision criterion

Choose a rule for comparing the alternatives.

The formal criteria are introduced in later weeks.

6. Payoff and loss formulations

The same sports decision can be expressed in different ways depending on the consequence being measured.

Cricket as a loss problem

If the consequence is runs conceded, smaller values are preferred.

Bowling decision Batter attacks Batter rotates strike
Fast bowler 12 7
Spin bowler 8 10
Medium pace 10 8

This is naturally interpreted as a loss table.

Cricket as a payoff problem

Suppose the analyst instead estimates the probability of successfully defending the target.

Bowling decision Batter attacks Batter rotates strike
Fast bowler 0.43 0.71
Spin bowler 0.64 0.56
Medium pace 0.51 0.63

Now larger values are preferred.

This is a payoff table.

The underlying decision problem may be similar, but the direction of preference changes with the consequence measure.

  • Runs conceded: lower is better.
  • Probability of winning: higher is better.

Suppose a 1500 m runner must select a race strategy before the event.

The likely race conditions are:

[ S_1= ]

[ S_2=. ]

Race strategy Fast race Slow tactical race
Fast start 222 230
Even pace 224 225
Conservative start 231 221

Here, smaller values are preferred.

Class questions

  1. What are the decision alternatives?
  2. What are the states of nature?
  3. What is the consequence?
  4. Which strategy is best if the race is known to be fast?
  5. Which strategy is best if the race is known to be slow?
  6. Can one strategy be selected before the race condition is known?

Answers

  1. Fast start, even pace, conservative start.
  2. Fast race and slow tactical race.
  3. Finishing time in seconds.
  4. Fast start, because 222 seconds is the smallest time.
  5. Conservative start, because 221 seconds is the smallest time.
  6. Not from this table alone. We still need a decision criterion.

The same athletics problem using a payoff

Suppose instead the analysts estimate the probability of finishing in the top three.

Race strategy Fast race Tactical race
Fast start 0.44 0.19
Even pace 0.35 0.33
Conservative start 0.21 0.49

Now larger values are preferred.

This immediately shows an important feature of decision problems:

The action that looks best depends on the state of nature.

There is no single strategy that dominates in both race conditions.

Before doing any calculation, write the problem in a structured form.

Template

For any sports decision problem, identify:

Decision maker

Who chooses the action?

Alternatives

[ A_1,A_2,,A_m ]

What can be chosen?

States of nature

[ S_1,S_2,,S_n ]

What uncertain future conditions may occur?

Consequences

[ C_{ij} ]

What happens if (A_i) is selected and (S_j) occurs?

Available information

What is currently known about the states of nature?

11. Why the distinction matters

A common mistake is to mix up an action with an event.

Consider:

“The batter attacks.”

That is not the captain’s decision. It is a possible state of nature.

Now consider:

“The captain selects the spinner.”

That is a decision alternative.

Similarly in athletics:

“The race becomes tactical.”

is a state of nature.

But:

“The athlete adopts an even pace.”

is a decision alternative.

The same distinction will matter throughout the course.
