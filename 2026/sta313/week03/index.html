WEEK 03

Maximax and Maximin Criteria

A sports decision often must be made before we know what conditions will arise. If reliable probabilities for those conditions are unavailable, expected-value calculations are not justified. This lesson introduces two contrasting decision rules: Maximax, which focuses on the best possible outcome, and Maximin, which protects against the worst possible outcome.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  1. Distinguish a decision under uncertainty from a decision under risk.
  2. Construct and interpret payoff and cost matrices for a sports decision.
  3. Apply Maximax and Maximin correctly to a payoff matrix.
  4. Apply the corresponding optimistic and conservative rules to a cost matrix without confusing maximization and minimization.
  5. Explain why two decision makers may select different actions from the same table.

1. Recall: Decisions under uncertainty

A decision problem involves alternatives (actions available to the decision maker), states of nature (future circumstances outside the decision maker’s control), and consequences (payoffs, losses, or costs).

Under uncertainty, the possible states are identified but no defensible probabilities are available. Thus, we do not assign arbitrary probabilities or compute expected monetary values.

Decision structure

Action + State of nature → Consequence

Payoff table: larger values are preferred.

Cost table: smaller values are preferred.

2. Maximax: The optimistic criterion for payoffs

Suppose a cricket captain must select one batter to send in during the final overs of a T20 match. The opposition may use a pace-dominated, spin-dominated, or mixed bowling attack, but the captain has no defensible probabilities for these possibilities.

The table shows the runs each batter could score under the three bowling approaches. The figures are hypothetical outcomes used to illustrate decision criteria, not predictions or probabilities. Because the entries are runs scored, larger payoffs are better.

Batter Pace-dominated attack Spin-dominated attack Mixed attack
\(A_1\): Batter A 32 14 10
\(A_2\): Batter B 24 20 16
\(A_3\): Batter C 26 23 19

Maximax selects the action with the largest row maximum.

For a payoff \(P_{ij}\), where \(i\) indexes the batter selected and \(j\) the opposition’s bowling approach:

\[ A^*_{\mathrm{Maximax}} = \arg\max_i \left\{\max_j P_{ij}\right\}. \]

Step 1: Find the highest number of runs each batter could score.

Batter Row maximum (runs)
Batter A 32
Batter B 24
Batter C 26

Step 2: Choose the largest row maximum.

\[ \max\{32,24,26\}=32. \]

Maximax selects Batter A, whose best possible outcome is 32 runs against a pace-dominated attack.

Interpretation: Maximax focuses on the highest attainable score. It does not mean that a pace-dominated attack is more likely than the other bowling approaches.

Ask the class: Would Batter A still be attractive if the opposition used a mixed bowling attack, against which Batter A could score only 10 runs? What does Maximax overlook?

Discussion point: Maximax ignores Batter A’s lower payoffs in the other states. A captain concerned about a poor outcome might prefer a different batter.

3. Maximin: The conservative criterion for payoffs

Using the same cricket payoff table, Maximin selects the batter whose worst possible runs scored are greatest.

\[ A^*_{\mathrm{Maximin}} = \arg\max_i \left\{\min_j P_{ij}\right\}. \]

Step 1: Find the lowest number of runs each batter could score.

Batter Row minimum (runs)
Batter A 10
Batter B 16
Batter C 19

Step 2: Choose the largest of the row minima.

\[ \max\{10,16,19\}=19. \]

Maximin selects Batter C, whose lowest projected score is 19 runs across the three listed bowling approaches, assuming the table describes the possible outcomes accurately.

Maximin is conservative because it evaluates each batter by the least favorable outcome. It can be appropriate when avoiding a very low score matters more than achieving the highest possible score.

Compare the two decisions

Criterion How each batter is evaluated Selected batter
Maximax Highest runs scored in each row Batter A
Maximin Lowest runs scored in each row Batter C

Neither rule uses probabilities. They reflect different decision attitudes, not different estimates of which bowling approach is most likely.

4. Cost matrices: An athletics example

An athletics coach must choose a training plan for a 100 m sprinter before an important competition. The conditions on race day are uncertain. The table gives the athlete’s projected 100 m finishing time (in seconds) under three possible conditions. The times are hypothetical and used for teaching purposes. Smaller values are better.

Training plan Dry track Wet track Windy conditions
\(A_1\): Speed-focused 10.40 11.10 10.90
\(A_2\): Endurance-focused 10.80 10.80 10.90
\(A_3\): Balanced 10.60 10.70 10.80

Objective: Choose a training plan that minimizes the athlete’s 100 m finishing time. Unlike runs scored in the cricket example, finishing time is a cost-type consequence: a lower value is preferable.

Important: Do not apply the payoff Maximax and Maximin formulas unchanged to a cost table. With finishing times, lower values are preferred.

4.1 Optimistic choice for costs: Minimin

An optimistic coach considers the fastest possible time for each training plan and selects the smallest of these times.

\[ A^*_{\mathrm{Minimin}} = \arg\min_i \left\{\min_j C_{ij}\right\}. \]

Training plan Minimum time (seconds)
Speed-focused 10.40
Endurance-focused 10.80
Balanced 10.60

\[ \min\{10.40,10.80,10.60\}=10.40. \]

Minimin selects the speed-focused plan, whose best-case finishing time is 10.40 seconds.

4.2 Conservative choice for costs: Minimax cost

A conservative coach considers the slowest possible time for each plan and selects the plan with the smallest worst-case time.

\[ A^*_{\mathrm{Minimax\ cost}} = \arg\min_i \left\{\max_j C_{ij}\right\}. \]

Training plan Maximum time (seconds)
Speed-focused 11.10
Endurance-focused 10.90
Balanced 10.80

\[ \min\{11.10,10.90,10.80\}=10.80. \]

Minimax cost selects the balanced plan, whose worst-case finishing time is 10.80 seconds.

Ask the class: Why might a coach choose the balanced plan even though the speed-focused plan could produce the fastest race? The balanced plan limits the worst-case finishing time, whereas the speed-focused plan offers the fastest best-case result.

Note: Wind direction and strength, not merely the presence of wind, affect sprint times. The three columns are simplified hypothetical race-day scenarios rather than measured performance predictions.

Payoff versus cost: Keep the direction straight

Decision attitude Payoff (larger is better) Cost (smaller is better)
Optimistic Maximax: largest row maximum Minimin: smallest row minimum
Conservative Maximin: largest row minimum Minimax cost: smallest row maximum

These are equivalent ways to express optimism and conservatism when the consequences are coded as benefits versus costs. The term Minimax cost here refers to worst-case costs, not the minimax regret criterion introduced in Week 04.

5. Guided discussion: When might the criteria disagree?

Return to the cricket example. Batter A could score as many as 32 runs, but only 10 runs against a mixed bowling attack. Batter C’s best-case score is 26 runs, but the lowest score in Batter C’s row is 19 runs.

Discuss with a partner:

  1. If the team needs an exceptionally high score in the final overs to have a chance of winning, which batter might the captain select? Explain the optimistic reasoning.
  2. If the captain’s priority is to avoid a very low contribution, which batter might be preferable? Explain the conservative reasoning.
  3. Can you justify either decision without assuming one bowling approach is more probable than another?

Teaching point: Maximax recommends Batter A because of the 32-run best case. Maximin recommends Batter C because of the 19-run worst case. The appropriate rule depends on the team’s objective and how the captain evaluates unfavorable outcomes, not on invented probabilities.

6. Student activity: Volleyball serving strategy

A volleyball coach chooses among three serving strategies. The following values are net points gained per set under three possible receiving formations. Larger values are better. Probabilities are unavailable.

Strategy Formation 1 Formation 2 Formation 3
\(A_1\): Power serve 11 2 5
\(A_2\): Float serve 7 6 4
\(A_3\): Targeted serve 9 5 6

Tasks (6 minutes)

  1. Identify the alternatives and states of nature.
  2. Compute each strategy’s row maximum and choose by Maximax.
  3. Compute each strategy’s row minimum and choose by Maximin.
  4. Explain the difference in one or two sentences.
NoteInstructor solution

Row maxima: power 11, float 7, targeted 9. Maximax chooses power serve.

Row minima: power 2, float 4, targeted 5. Maximin chooses targeted serve.

The optimistic rule emphasizes the highest potential net gain. The conservative rule emphasizes protection against the least favorable receiving formation.

7. Common mistakes to avoid

  • Mixing up payoffs and costs. First identify whether large or small values are desirable.
  • Choosing the largest individual payoff for Maximin. Maximin first finds the minimum in each row.
  • Assigning probabilities without evidence. That changes the decision setting and requires justification.
  • Interpreting Maximax as a prediction. It selects the most attractive best case, not the most likely outcome.
  • Confusing Minimax cost with Minimax Regret. Regret requires a separate opportunity-loss table, covered next week.

8. Exit check (4 minutes)

A team chooses between two tactics with payoffs:

Tactic State 1 State 2
\(A\) 15 1
\(B\) 9 7

Question 1: Which tactic is selected by Maximax?

Question 2: Which tactic is selected by Maximin?

Question 3: If these numbers were costs instead of payoffs, what would the optimistic and conservative choices be?

TipAnswers
  1. Maximax: \(A\), because \(15 > 9\).
  2. Maximin: \(B\), because \(7 > 1\).
  3. Minimin cost: \(A\), because \(1 < 7\). Minimax cost: \(B\), because \(9 < 15\).

Independent learning

Create one payoff matrix and one cost matrix for a sports decision of your choice, each with at least three alternatives and three states. Apply both optimistic and conservative criteria. Explain the selected action in words, including why no probabilities were used.
